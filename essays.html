No. 01

Why Is This Teapot Beautiful?

Object — Ceramic — On Composition

Add photo
lutz-teapot-01.jpg

I saw this teapot in the window of a store in Barcelona. I had already passed it by a few metres when something made me stop. No, not stop. Go back. I looked at it again and thought: this is beautiful. And then almost immediately: this is incredibly cute. Or, as a Japanese girl might call it, kawaii.

The teapot stayed in my mind. I kept thinking about it, and now I am writing this text. Basically I want to know what happens between seeing an object and experiencing it as beautiful.

George Santayana, in The Sense of Beauty, makes an interesting distinction. Beauty is not really a physical quality of an object. It is a pleasure that we experience in relation to it — a pleasure that becomes so attached to the object that we perceive the object itself as beautiful.

When we look at a painting, we rarely say, I am experiencing pleasure looking at this painting. We say, this painting is beautiful. So the pleasure has somehow been transferred onto the object.

What is this pleasure made of? What is the material of beauty? Colour, sound, shape, texture, taste… The sensory experience of an object as the most straightforward layer of perception.

What I notice first about this teapot is its softness. Not actual softness, the ceramic is hard, but visual softness. Its rounded body feels almost touchable and cozy. It isn't perfectly round, either. It doesn't seem interested in geometric perfection. It looks a little like a cloud, or perhaps a small boat.

The lid continues this feeling. It sits on top almost like a deck on that boat. It doesn't feel like a separate component placed onto the body. It seems to grow out of the same shape, as if the whole thing had been formed in one continuous gesture.

And then there is the thing that makes my eye stop: the wooden bar. One thin, straight line crossing this soft, rounded body. This is where the object becomes interesting. Until now, I have been looking at its individual sensory qualities, but there is another layer to it: composition, or the relationship between these elements.

There is almost a contradiction between the softness of the body and the rigidity of the wooden stick. Between the heavy, quiet mass of the teapot and this delicate horizontal line floating above it. The ceramic body needs the wooden line, and the wooden line needs the softness of the body around it. They make each other more noticeable.

If we flatten the teapot into a two-dimensional composition, the structure becomes even clearer. There is one large oval — the body — with a smaller oval inside it, the lid, then the small wooden form and, finally, one straight horizontal line: the handle. Round, round, round — line. The repetition creates rhythm; the line interrupts it.

There is unity, but also contrast. The composition is simple, but not boring. There is nothing to remove.

I think I figured out part of the mystery of this teapot. And I think I know what I want for my birthday. Pleasure shaped in ceramic, held together by a bit of wood, making a little sea of tea to sail away in.

No. 02

On the Weight of a Matcha Bowl

Object — Ceramic — Ritual

A good chawan is heavier than you expect the first time you hold it, and that is the entire point. The weight slows your hands down before the tea does anything to your head. Most packaging today is designed to disappear — light, frictionless, gone in one motion. A bowl built for matcha does the opposite: it insists on a pause.

What I keep noticing, watching people discover matcha as a category rather than a drink, is that the ritual sells the product more honestly than the marketing does. You can't rush a whisked bowl. The beauty is in the resistance.

I think about this every time I sit in a brand review and someone asks how we can make the "experience" faster. Sometimes faster is the wrong axis entirely. The chawan works precisely because it refuses to be optimized — it asks for two hands, a stable surface, your attention. In a category built on wellness claims, that physical demand for presence is more persuasive than any ingredient callout.

The lesson I keep bringing back to matcha as a brand: don't design the friction out. Design the right friction in.

No. 03

The Discipline of Empty Space

Practice — Ikebana — Restraint

In ikebana, the branch you remove matters more than the one you keep. Beginners fill the vessel; the practice is learning to leave it alone. What looks like emptiness is actually the hardest decision in the arrangement — the negative space is doing as much work as the flower.

I think about this constantly in brand work, where the instinct is always to add one more line, one more offer, one more banner. The strongest campaigns I've shipped were the ones I fought to keep empty.

There's a specific moment in every arrangement where you've placed three branches and the urge hits to add a fourth "just in case it looks unfinished." That urge is almost always wrong. The same moment happens in a campaign brief — the extra CTA, the second discount banner, the third value prop crammed above the fold. Ikebana trained me to notice that moment and sit with the discomfort of leaving it alone.

Empty space isn't the absence of a decision. It's the decision.

No. 04

Why a Kombucha Bottle Deserves a Second Look

Object — Glass — Category

Launching a fermented drink in a new city means fighting for shelf space against products people already trust. The bottle has to do the convincing before anyone reads the label. What I learned launching MOR'KO in Hamburg is that glass has a memory — the thickness, the neck taper, the way light sits in it — and shoppers read that memory instinctively, faster than copy.

Beauty, in a fridge full of competitors, is a shortcut to trust.

Before a single flavor was finalized, we spent weeks on the bottle itself — weight in hand, how the cap sounded when it cracked open, how the label wrapped around the curve instead of sitting flat on it. None of that shows up in a tasting note. All of it shows up in the first three seconds someone spends deciding whether to trust a brand they've never heard of, standing in a fridge in a city where they've never heard of us either.

That's the part of "brand" that never makes it into the deck: the object has to argue on your behalf before you're in the room.

No. 05

The Kerning of a Title Card

Film — Typography — First Impressions

Before a single frame of story, a film tells you how much it respects you through its opening title. Loose kerning, a lazy stock font, a rushed fade — you know within four seconds what kind of attention went into the rest. The directors I return to are, without exception, the ones who treated the title card like a promise.

Brands make the same promise in their first five seconds. Most break it immediately.

I notice it most when it's absent — a beautifully shot film that opens on a title card clearly built by whoever was free that afternoon, default tracking, default weight, no relationship to anything that follows. It's a small thing, and it tells you everything about how decisions got made on that production.

The same test applies to a landing page, an email header, a shipping box. The first typographic choice a person sees is a promise about the care behind every choice after it. Most brands don't realize they're making that promise. The good ones never break it.
